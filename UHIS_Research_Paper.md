## Abstract

Urban Heat Islands (UHIs) constitute one of the most consequential environmental phenomena of the twenty-first century, wherein urbanised areas exhibit land surface temperatures (LST) that are 1–12°C higher than surrounding rural landscapes due to the replacement of natural land cover with impervious materials. As the global urban population surpasses 56% and is projected to reach 68% by 2050, the health, energy, and ecological consequences of UHI effects demand scalable, intelligent detection and mitigation platforms. However, existing remote sensing–based UHI monitoring approaches suffer from five critical deficiencies: (1) physics-blind fixed temperature thresholds that conflate geographic climate baselines with genuine urban thermal anomalies; (2) insufficient feature spaces limited to two or three spectral indices; (3) spatial autocorrelation leakage arising from random train–test splits that allow data from the same city to appear in both partitions; (4) black-box classification that provides no explanation of which environmental factors drive a UHI prediction; and (5) the absence of any actionable counterfactual feedback mechanism for urban planners.

This paper presents the Urban Heat Intelligence System (UHIS), a production-deployed, web-based geospatial intelligence platform that integrates real-time multi-satellite data from four constellations (Landsat 8/9, Sentinel-2, NASA SRTM, NOAA VIIRS) via Google Earth Engine with an Explainable AI pipeline built on XGBoost and SHAP TreeExplainer. The system synthesises seven spectral, topographic, and anthropogenic features extracted concurrently from a 4,882-sample global dataset spanning 62 cities across 6 climate zones. The XGBoost classifier achieves 93.49% accuracy and 0.9850 ROC-AUC under strict spatial cross-validation (GroupKFold by city), while the Random Forest regressor explains 94.16% of temperature variance (R² = 0.9416, RMSE = ±2.08°C). An integrated counterfactual simulation engine enables real-time what-if analysis of urban planning interventions grounded in peer-reviewed empirical bounds. UHIS addresses all five identified gaps through a novel lst_delta–based climate-zone-adaptive labelling methodology, expanded seven-dimensional feature vectors, rigorous spatial cross-validation, per-prediction SHAP explanations, and literature-grounded mitigation simulation — delivering a complete, self-contained pipeline from satellite observation to actionable urban planning recommendation.

**Keywords:** Urban Heat Island, Remote Sensing, XGBoost, SHAP, Explainable AI, Google Earth Engine, Land Surface Temperature, Counterfactual Simulation, Geospatial Machine Learning, Climate Adaptation

## I. Introduction

The Urban Heat Island (UHI) phenomenon refers to the well-documented observation that metropolitan areas experience significantly elevated temperatures relative to their surrounding rural counterparts. This temperature differential arises from the systematic modification of land surfaces during urbanisation, wherein natural vegetation and permeable soils are replaced with impervious materials — concrete, asphalt, glass, and metal roofing — that absorb, store, and re-radiate solar energy at substantially higher rates than their natural equivalents [1], [5]. The magnitude of the UHI effect is nontrivial: temperature differentials of 1–12°C have been recorded across diverse climatic settings, with nocturnal intensities frequently exceeding daytime values due to the slow release of stored thermal energy from urban thermal mass [6].

The consequences of UHI extend well beyond thermal discomfort. Epidemiological research has established that heat-related mortality in urban areas increases by 1–3% per degree Celsius above local baseline temperatures, with vulnerable populations — the elderly, children, and outdoor labourers — disproportionately affected [7]. Energy demand for artificial cooling increases by 5–10% per 1°C of UHI intensity, imposing significant economic and carbon costs on urban infrastructure [2]. Furthermore, elevated urban temperatures accelerate ground-level ozone formation, degrade urban air quality, and contribute to thermal pollution of surface water runoff. With the global urban population having surpassed 4.4 billion and projections indicating that 68% of the world population will reside in cities by 2050 [15], the development of scalable, intelligent systems for UHI detection and mitigation is an urgent research and policy priority.

Despite the growing availability of satellite remote sensing data — Landsat, Sentinel-2, MODIS, and VIIRS programmes now provide global thermal and spectral imagery at resolutions ranging from 10 to 1,000 metres — existing UHI detection and analysis approaches are constrained by five critical deficiencies. First, the majority of deployed systems utilise physics-blind fixed absolute temperature thresholds to flag UHI events (e.g., LST > 35°C), failing to distinguish between a city in the Sahara where 45°C represents ambient summer conditions and a temperate European city where 35°C constitutes a genuine anomaly [7]. Second, many approaches rely on an insufficient feature space of only two to three spectral indices — typically LST and NDVI — thereby omitting anthropogenic heat drivers such as nighttime energy emissions and topographic cooling gradients that are essential for accurate classification across diverse geographic contexts [6]. Third, the use of random train–test partitioning in geospatial machine learning introduces spatial autocorrelation leakage, wherein data points from the same city appear in both training and test sets, artificially inflating reported accuracy by as much as 10–20 percentage points [12]. Fourth, existing systems operate as black-box classifiers, providing a binary UHI/not-UHI label without decomposing which specific environmental factors — vegetation loss, built-up density, elevation deficit, anthropogenic heat emission — drove the classification, a critical gap for urban planners who require not merely detection but causal understanding [4]. Fifth, no prior operational system integrates counterfactual simulation capabilities that allow planners to explore what-if scenarios — quantifying, for instance, the thermal impact of increasing urban tree canopy by 40% or deploying cool roofing over 60% of available surfaces before committing resources [1], [2], [3].

The Urban Heat Intelligence System (UHIS) presented in this paper addresses all five limitations through a one-to-one mapping of architectural innovations. The lst_delta–based climate-zone-adaptive labelling methodology replaces fixed thresholds with a relative thermal anomaly metric calibrated to six distinct climatic regimes. The feature space is expanded to seven dimensions spanning spectral, topographic, and anthropogenic domains. GroupKFold cross-validation by city prevents spatial leakage. Real-time SHAP TreeExplainer provides exact per-prediction feature attributions. A dual-mode counterfactual simulation engine grounded in peer-reviewed empirical bounds enables interactive what-if analysis. The system is deployed as a production-grade web application with a high-concurrency FastAPI backend and a Three.js/React-based mission-control frontend.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section II reviews related work in remote sensing UHI detection, explainable AI for geospatial applications, and mitigation simulation. Section III describes the three-tier system architecture. Section IV details the dataset construction and feature engineering pipeline. Section V presents the machine learning pipeline including hyperparameter optimisation and spatial cross-validation. Section VI describes the SHAP-based explainable AI engine. Section VII details the counterfactual mitigation simulation engine. Section VIII reports comprehensive evaluation results and comparative analysis. Section IX concludes with contributions and future directions.

## II. Related Work

### A. Remote Sensing for UHI Detection

The application of thermal remote sensing to urban climate studies has a substantial lineage. Voogt and Oke [5] provided a foundational review of thermal remote sensing for urban climates, establishing that satellite-derived LST serves as a reliable proxy for surface-level UHI effects. Their work, however, predated the operational deployment of machine learning classifiers and was limited to static thermal mapping without automated detection capabilities. Zhou et al. [6] advanced the field by applying Random Forest classification to MODIS-derived LST time series for surface UHI characterisation, demonstrating the viability of tree-ensemble methods for UHI detection. Nevertheless, their approach utilised only three spectral features and did not address spatial autocorrelation in cross-validation, producing accuracy estimates that may overstate generalisation to unseen cities. Deilami et al. [7] conducted a comprehensive systematic review of spatio-temporal UHI factors, data sources, and mitigation measures, synthesising 75 studies to identify key drivers including vegetation loss, impervious surface expansion, and building density. Their review, however, identified a critical gap: no operational system in the reviewed literature combined automated ML classification, causal explanation, and interactive mitigation simulation into a single deployed platform.

More recent work has leveraged deep learning approaches for UHI mapping. Huang et al. [11] applied convolutional neural networks to Landsat imagery for urban thermal pattern recognition, achieving improved spatial resolution but at the cost of interpretability. Kafy et al. [13] combined cellular automata with multi-temporal Landsat analysis for UHI prediction under land-use change scenarios, though their approach lacked real-time inference capability and provided no per-prediction explanation.

### B. Explainable AI in Geospatial Machine Learning

Lundberg and Lee [4] introduced SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) at NeurIPS 2017, providing a unified framework for interpreting model predictions grounded in game-theoretic Shapley values. The key theoretical contribution is that SHAP values represent the unique additive feature attribution method satisfying local accuracy, missingness, and consistency — three desirable axiomatic properties. The TreeExplainer algorithm, a specialisation for tree-ensemble models, computes exact Shapley values in polynomial time rather than the exponential time required by the general formulation, making real-time per-prediction explanation computationally feasible for production deployment. This contrasts fundamentally with Gini importance, which provides only a global average ranking of feature contributions across the entire training set and cannot explain why a specific prediction was made for a specific location. In the context of urban planning, this distinction is critical: a planner assessing a specific neighbourhood requires localised causal attribution — which features are driving the UHI classification at this particular coordinate — rather than a dataset-wide average. Ribeiro et al. [14] introduced LIME as an alternative local explanation approach; however, LIME relies on local linear approximation and sampling perturbation, which introduces stochastic variability and does not guarantee exact attribution values for tree models.

### C. Mitigation Simulation in UHI Research

The empirical literature on UHI mitigation provides well-established cooling bounds for specific interventions. Bowler et al. [1] conducted a meta-analysis of urban greening interventions, reporting mean cooling effects of 1–4°C for urban tree canopy expansion. Santamouris [2] reviewed reflective and green roof technologies, documenting maximum cooling potentials of 5.5°C for high-albedo cool roofing and 2.5°C for green roof installations. Taha [3] quantified the cooling effects of increased albedo and water body proximity, reporting 1–2.5°C reductions. Despite this robust empirical evidence base, no prior operational system has translated these literature-derived cooling bounds into an interactive, real-time counterfactual simulation engine integrated with a live satellite data pipeline and ML classification system.

The research gap that UHIS addresses is therefore threefold: (a) existing systems do not combine real-time satellite feature extraction with explainable ML classification; (b) no deployed platform provides per-prediction causal attribution via exact SHAP values; and (c) no prior system integrates literature-grounded counterfactual mitigation simulation as a first-class subsystem within a production geospatial intelligence platform.

## III. System Architecture

The UHIS employs a three-tier architecture separating presentation, application logic, and data/intelligence concerns, designed for low-latency concurrent satellite data processing and real-time ML inference.

The Presentation Tier is implemented in React 18 with Vite 5 as the build system, delivering a mission-control-style interface. Three.js and React Three Fiber (R3F) power three-dimensional WebGL data visualisations including holographic metric displays and an interactive globe. Apache ECharts renders high-performance SHAP waterfall charts and telemetry time-series. Leaflet provides the interactive geospatial map with live Google Earth Engine tile overlays for LST, NDVI, NDBI, and NTL layers. Framer Motion and GSAP orchestrate physics-based entrance animations and cinematic transitions throughout the interface.

The Application Tier is built on FastAPI, a high-performance asynchronous Python REST framework running on Uvicorn ASGI server. The backend is decomposed into six microservice modules, each with a single responsibility: GEE Service (569 lines) handles authentication and parallel satellite data fetching; ML Service (437 lines) manages model loading, feature vector construction, XGBoost/RF inference, and SHAP computation; Analysis Service (159 lines) classifies environmental metrics into human-readable severity levels; Recommendation Service (135 lines) maps detected causes to actionable mitigation strategies; Simulation Service (89 lines) calculates counterfactual temperatures; and Thresholds Configuration (60 lines) centralises all classification thresholds, satellite parameters, and simulation bounds. All request and response payloads are validated through Pydantic v2 schemas providing type-safe serialisation and automatic API documentation.

The Data and Intelligence Tier integrates three external and internal data systems. Google Earth Engine provides cloud-computed access to four satellite constellations (Landsat 8/9, Sentinel-2, NASA SRTM, NOAA VIIRS). The trained ML models — an XGBoost classifier and Random Forest regressor — are loaded from serialised joblib artefacts at startup. SHAP TreeExplainer operates in-process on the loaded XGBoost model, computing exact Shapley values during inference.

A critical performance optimisation is the concurrent satellite data extraction pipeline. The GEE Service employs a ThreadPoolExecutor with five parallel worker threads, each assigned to a distinct satellite data source: Thread 1 fetches Landsat LST, Thread 2 fetches Sentinel-2 spectral indices (NDVI, NDBI, EVI), Thread 3 fetches SRTM elevation, Thread 4 fetches VIIRS nighttime light radiance, and Thread 5 computes the rural baseline LST from a 3–15 km annular buffer. This parallelisation reduces end-to-end data extraction latency from 20–40 seconds (sequential) to 8–12 seconds (bounded by the slowest thread). Each thread operates within an independent try/except fault-tolerance wrapper with predefined fallback values (e.g., lst_celsius = 34.5°C, ndvi = 0.16, elevation = 50.0 m), ensuring graceful degradation when individual satellite products are temporarily unavailable. The response includes a data_source provenance field indicating whether results derive from live GEE data, partial fallback, or full fallback.

## IV. Dataset and Feature Engineering

### A. Multi-Modal Satellite Data Sources

The system extracts environmental features from four satellite constellations via the Google Earth Engine cloud compute infrastructure. Landsat 8 and Landsat 9 (USGS) provide thermal infrared imagery at 30 m spatial resolution through the Collection 2 Level-2 surface temperature product (LANDSAT/LC08/C02/T1_L2 and LANDSAT/LC09/C02/T1_L2), from which Land Surface Temperature (LST) is derived using band ST_B10. Sentinel-2 (ESA Copernicus) provides multi-spectral imagery at 10 m resolution through the harmonised surface reflectance product (COPERNICUS/S2_SR_HARMONIZED), from which three spectral indices — NDVI, NDBI, and EVI — are computed using bands B2 (Blue), B4 (Red), B8 (NIR), and B11 (SWIR). NASA SRTM (USGS/SRTMGL1_003) provides a 30 m digital elevation model yielding topographic elevation in metres. NOAA VIIRS Day/Night Band monthly composites (NOAA/VIIRS/DNB/MONTHLY_V1/VCMSLCFG) provide nighttime light radiance at 500 m resolution via the avg_rad band, serving as a proxy for anthropogenic energy emissions.

### B. LST Computation

Land Surface Temperature is derived from the Landsat Collection 2 Level-2 surface temperature product through the standard scaling transformation: LST_Kelvin = ST_B10 × 0.00341802 + 149.0, followed by conversion to Celsius: LST_Celsius = LST_Kelvin − 273.15. The system first queries Landsat 8; if no imagery meeting the cloud cover threshold is available for the region of interest, it transparently falls back to Landsat 9 to maximise temporal coverage.

### C. Spectral Index Computation

Three spectral indices are computed from Sentinel-2 surface reflectance bands. NDVI (Normalised Difference Vegetation Index) is calculated as (NIR − Red) / (NIR + Red) using bands B8 and B4, serving as a vegetation density indicator inversely correlated with impervious surface coverage. NDBI (Normalised Difference Built-up Index) is calculated as (SWIR − NIR) / (SWIR + NIR) using bands B11 and B8, serving as a direct proxy for built-up area and urban thermal mass. EVI (Enhanced Vegetation Index) is calculated as 2.5 × (NIR − Red) / (NIR + 6 × Red − 7.5 × Blue + 1) using bands B8, B4, and B2, providing a vegetation signal that is more robust than NDVI in high-biomass and dense canopy regions. Sentinel-2 surface reflectance values are stored as integers scaled by a factor of 10,000; the system normalises by dividing by 10,000 before applying the EVI formula, and EVI values are subsequently clipped to the physically plausible range [−1, 2] to handle edge-pixel artefacts.

### D. Annular Rural Buffer Innovation

A critical methodological innovation is the annular rural buffer computation for deriving a spatially contextualised thermal baseline. For each urban coordinate, an annular ring is constructed with an inner exclusion radius of 3 km (8 km during training) and an outer sampling radius of 15 km: rural_ring = point.buffer(15 km).difference(point.buffer(3 km)). The mean LST within this annulus (rural_lst_mean) represents the surrounding rural thermal baseline, and the key derived feature lst_delta is computed as lst_delta = lst_celsius − rural_lst_mean. This formulation ensures that lst_delta captures the true thermal anomaly attributable to urbanisation rather than the geographic climate baseline — a desert city with an ambient temperature of 45°C and a rural baseline of 43°C yields an lst_delta of only 2°C, correctly reflecting a modest UHI effect, whereas the same absolute temperature in a temperate setting might yield an lst_delta of 8–10°C.

### E. Climate-Zone-Adaptive Labelling

Rather than applying a fixed global threshold for UHI classification, the system implements climate-zone-specific lst_delta thresholds calibrated to the thermal characteristics of six climatic regimes. The Arid zone threshold is set at 3.5°C, reflecting the extreme ambient heat of desert cities that requires a higher bar for UHI detection. The Tropical zone threshold is 2.5°C, accounting for high baseline humidity that moderates relative thermal anomalies. The Subtropical and Mediterranean zones share a 2.0°C threshold representing a standard mid-latitude baseline. The Temperate zone employs the lowest threshold of 1.5°C, where even modest temperature elevations above rural baselines constitute a meaningful UHI signal. The Monsoon zone uses a 2.5°C threshold to accommodate seasonal precipitation effects on thermal dynamics. The binary UHI label is assigned as uhi_label = 1 if lst_delta ≥ zone_threshold, and 0 otherwise.

### F. Dataset Construction Statistics

The training dataset was constructed using a dedicated collection script (collect_dataset.py, 439 lines) that sampled 80 points per city across 62 cities spanning all inhabited continents, yielding a total of 4,882 samples after quality filtering. The 62 cities span 6 climate zones: Arid (e.g., Cairo, Dubai, Phoenix, Riyadh), Tropical (e.g., Lagos, Bangkok, Singapore, Jakarta), Subtropical (e.g., Mumbai, São Paulo, Houston), Temperate (e.g., London, Berlin, Tokyo, Seoul), Mediterranean (e.g., Barcelona, Rome, Athens), and Monsoon (e.g., Delhi, Kolkata, Dhaka, Chennai). The temporal window covers March through July (peak Northern Hemisphere heating season) across four years (2021–2024), with a maximum cloud cover filter of 20%. The resulting dataset exhibits a UHI positive rate of 44.94%, representing a well-balanced binary classification problem that does not require aggressive oversampling or class-weight manipulation.

### G. Feature Vector Design

The classifier employs six features: lst_delta (urban-rural thermal differential), ndvi (vegetation density), ndbi (built-up density), evi (enhanced vegetation), elevation (topographic altitude), and ntl (nighttime light radiance). A critical design decision is the exclusion of raw LST from the classifier feature vector. Since the UHI label is derived from lst − rural_lst_mean, including raw lst as a predictor would introduce label leakage through circular dependency, enabling the classifier to achieve artificially high accuracy by learning a trivial threshold function. Instead, lst_delta — the relative anomaly — provides the actual signal the model must learn to distinguish. The regressor employs seven features: ndvi, ndbi, evi, elevation, ntl, abs_lat (absolute latitude as a solar irradiance proxy), and rural_lst_mean (regional climate baseline). The regressor predicts absolute LST from environmental and geographic features, enabling counterfactual temperature estimation when spectral indices are modified by simulated interventions.

### H. Preprocessing Pipeline

Feature preprocessing includes StandardScaler normalisation applied to classifier inputs (XGBoost benefits from normalised features during hyperparameter search), EVI clipping to [−1, 2], LST sanity checking with samples outside [−10°C, 80°C] discarded, and NDVI/NDBI range validation with values outside [−1, 1] excluded. When auxiliary features (EVI, elevation, NTL) are unavailable for a sample during inference, sensible defaults are applied: EVI is approximated as NDVI × 0.9, elevation defaults to 0.0 m, and NTL defaults to 0.0 nW/cm²/sr.

## V. Machine Learning Pipeline

### A. XGBoost Classifier

The UHI classifier is implemented using XGBClassifier (XGBoost v3.2.0) [8] with a binary logistic objective and log-loss evaluation metric. Hyperparameter optimisation is performed via RandomizedSearchCV with 30 iterations, 5-fold cross-validation, and F1-score as the optimisation target. The hyperparameter search space spans eight dimensions: n_estimators ∈ {100, 200, 300, 500}, max_depth ∈ {3, 5, 7, 9}, learning_rate ∈ {0.01, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20}, subsample ∈ {0.7, 0.8, 0.9, 1.0}, colsample_bytree ∈ {0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 1.0}, min_child_weight ∈ {1, 3, 5}, gamma ∈ {0, 0.1, 0.2}, and scale_pos_weight ∈ {1, class_ratio}. The scale_pos_weight parameter is included to explore automatic class imbalance handling, computed as the ratio of negative to positive samples.

The cross-validation strategy employs GroupKFold with city name as the grouping variable, ensuring that entire cities are held out as complete units during each fold. Concretely, if Delhi, Lagos, and Tokyo appear in the test fold, no training data from these cities contaminates the training set for that fold. This prevents spatial autocorrelation leakage — a well-documented source of inflated accuracy in geospatial ML — where proximate points within the same urban area share correlated environmental signatures that enable trivial memorisation rather than genuine pattern generalisation. The resulting validation regime is significantly more challenging than random splitting: the model must generalise to entirely unseen urban environments with potentially different climatic regimes, building densities, and vegetation patterns. The reported accuracy of 93.49% under this strict protocol therefore represents a conservative, honest estimate of real-world generalisation performance.

### B. Random Forest Regressor

The temperature prediction model is implemented as a RandomForestRegressor (scikit-learn v1.8.0) configured with n_estimators = 300 trees, max_depth = None (unlimited depth allowing full tree growth), and min_samples_leaf = 2. The regressor is trained on a standard 80/20 random split (stratification being inapplicable to continuous regression targets). Its primary purpose within the UHIS pipeline is twofold: providing a continuous LST prediction used for UHI severity scoring, and enabling counterfactual temperature estimation during mitigation simulation by predicting the expected LST under modified spectral index values.

### C. Model Serialisation

All trained artefacts are persisted via joblib for efficient loading at service startup. The XGBoost classifier (uhi_classifier.pkl, approximately 178 KB) encapsulates the complete trained ensemble. The Random Forest regressor (temp_regressor.pkl, approximately 44 MB) stores 300 fully-grown decision trees. The StandardScaler (scaler.pkl, approximately 0.7 KB) preserves the training-set feature means and standard deviations for consistent inference-time normalisation.

### D. Feature-Count-Aware Inference

To support backward compatibility across model versions, the inference service dynamically detects the expected feature dimensionality at startup by probing model.n_features_in_. If the loaded classifier expects three features (version 1), the service constructs a three-element feature vector [lst, ndvi, ndbi]; if it expects six features (version 2), the full vector [lst_delta, ndvi, ndbi, evi, elevation, ntl] is assembled. This architectural decision enables zero-downtime model upgrades and facilitates A/B testing across model versions in production.

## VI. Explainable AI Engine

### A. SHAP TreeExplainer

The explainability subsystem employs SHAP (v0.45.0) [4], specifically the TreeExplainer algorithm, which computes exact Shapley values for tree-ensemble models in polynomial time. Unlike methods based on random perturbation or linear approximation, TreeExplainer exploits the recursive tree structure to compute exact path-based contributions, ensuring that the sum of all feature SHAP values plus the base value exactly equals the model's raw prediction output. The base value represents the model's expected prediction in log-odds space prior to observing any feature values — it is the average model output over the training distribution. During inference, the system instantiates a TreeExplainer on the loaded XGBoost classifier and computes SHAP values for each incoming feature vector: explainer = shap.TreeExplainer(xgb_classifier); shap_values = explainer.shap_values(X_scaled); base_value = explainer.expected_value.

### B. Per-Prediction SHAP Output

For each classification, the system produces a SHAP decomposition consisting of the base value and per-feature contributions in log-odds space. An illustrative example from a high-severity UHI location yields the following attribution: lst_delta = +2.41 (the urban-rural thermal differential strongly pushed toward UHI classification), ndbi = +1.13 (high built-up density contributed substantially), ntl = +0.21 (anthropogenic heat emission provided a moderate positive signal), ndvi = −0.32 (the existing vegetation provided partial cooling counterbalance), evi = −0.15 (dense canopy vegetation provided additional cooling), elevation = −0.09 (negligible topographic effect at this location), with a base_value of −0.50. The sum of these contributions (2.41 + 1.13 + 0.21 − 0.32 − 0.15 − 0.09 − 0.50 = 2.69 in log-odds) corresponds to a high UHI probability after sigmoid transformation. This level of per-prediction granularity enables urban planners to identify the precise environmental drivers at a specific location, rather than relying on global average importances that may not reflect local conditions.

### C. Global Gini Feature Importance

Complementing the local SHAP analysis, the system also reports global Gini importance computed from the trained XGBoost classifier. The classifier importance ranking is: lst_delta at 81.93%, elevation at 4.80%, evi at 3.76%, ndbi at 3.38%, ndvi at 3.08%, and ntl at 3.06%. The overwhelming dominance of lst_delta (81.93%) confirms that the urban-rural thermal differential is the principal signal for UHI classification, with the remaining five features contributing secondary but collectively meaningful discriminative information. The regressor feature importance follows a qualitatively different pattern: rural_lst_mean dominates at 77.92%, followed by abs_lat at 4.48%, ndvi at 4.32%, ndbi at 4.28%, ntl at 4.00%, elevation at 3.03%, and evi at 1.97%. The regressor's reliance on rural_lst_mean is physically expected — the regional climate baseline is the strongest predictor of absolute urban temperature, with spectral and anthropogenic features modulating the urban thermal increment.

### D. Waterfall Chart Visualisation

The frontend renders SHAP values as an ECharts waterfall bar chart within the diagnostic panel. Each feature is displayed as a horizontal bar: red-shaded bars represent features that push the prediction toward UHI (positive SHAP value), while blue or green-shaded bars represent features that push away from UHI (negative SHAP value). The base value serves as the leftmost anchor, and features are stacked in descending order of absolute SHAP magnitude, enabling planners to immediately identify the dominant driver at any queried location.

### E. SHAP versus Gini Importance

The fundamental distinction between SHAP and Gini importance lies in scope and exactness. Gini importance provides a single global ranking averaged across all training predictions and cannot distinguish whether a feature's contribution at a specific location matches or diverges from the global average. SHAP TreeExplainer provides a unique local explanation for each individual prediction, revealing feature interactions and location-specific dynamics. For urban planning applications, where interventions must be targeted to specific neighbourhoods, local explanations are substantially more actionable than global averages.

## VII. Mitigation Simulation Engine

### A. Counterfactual Framing

The simulation engine implements a counterfactual what-if analysis framework, enabling planners to pose interventional queries of the form: "What would the temperature at this location be if specific mitigation actions were applied at a given intensity?" This approach is grounded in the empirical cooling bounds established by peer-reviewed urban climate literature, ensuring that simulated outcomes remain within physically plausible ranges rather than relying solely on model extrapolation.

### B. Supported Mitigation Actions

The engine supports four evidence-based mitigation actions, each parameterised with a maximum cooling potential, spectral index deltas, and a literature citation. Urban Tree Cover (trees) provides a maximum cooling of 4.0°C with Δndvi = +0.18 and Δndbi = −0.05, calibrated from the meta-analysis by Bowler et al. [1]. Cool/Reflective Roofing (cool_roof) provides a maximum cooling of 5.5°C with Δndvi = 0.0 (no vegetation change) and Δndbi = −0.15, derived from the comprehensive review by Santamouris [2]. Water Features (water) provide a maximum cooling of 2.5°C with Δndvi = +0.10 and Δndbi = −0.06, based on the empirical measurements by Taha [3]. Green Roofs and Vertical Gardens (green_roof) provide a maximum cooling of 2.5°C with Δndvi = +0.08 and Δndbi = −0.10, also from Santamouris [2]. The combined maximum theoretical cooling across all four actions at full intensity is 14.5°C, though the system enforces an absolute temperature floor of 15.0°C to prevent physically unrealistic sub-ambient predictions.

### C. Simulation Algorithm

The simulation algorithm applies linear intensity scaling to each selected action. For each active mitigation action, the cooling contribution is computed as cooling = max_cooling × (intensity / 100), where intensity is a user-controlled slider value ranging from 0 to 100. The total cooling reduction is accumulated across all active actions, and the resulting simulated temperature is computed as new_temperature = max(original_lst − Σcooling, 15.0°C). The spectral indices are correspondingly adjusted: ndvi_modified = ndvi + Σ(ndvi_delta × intensity/100) and ndbi_modified = ndbi + Σ(ndbi_delta × intensity/100), enabling the simulation to update both the temperature prediction and the visual spectral indicators.

### D. Dual Simulation Modes

The system provides two simulation modes that reflect complementary analytical perspectives. The literature-based mode (primary) applies the empirical cooling bounds deterministically, providing fast, reproducible, and conservatively bounded temperature projections anchored in published research. The ML recalculation mode (secondary) adjusts the spectral feature vector (NDVI, NDBI) with the simulated deltas and re-runs the Random Forest regressor to predict the new LST, capturing potential non-linear interactions between features but subject to model extrapolation risk when feature values are pushed beyond the training distribution. Urban planners are presented with both estimates, enabling informed decision-making that balances empirical grounding with non-linear modelling insights.

## VIII. Results and Evaluation

### A. Classifier Performance

The XGBoost classifier, evaluated under GroupKFold spatial cross-validation with city-level grouping, achieves the following metrics on the held-out test partition (approximately 977 samples, reflecting the last fold of a 5-fold GroupKFold split with a 44.94% positive rate in the full dataset). The overall accuracy is 93.49%, indicating that fewer than 7 out of every 100 predictions are incorrect. Precision is 91.00%, meaning that 91 out of every 100 locations flagged as UHI hotspots are genuinely experiencing significant urban heat effects, with a false positive rate of approximately 9%. Recall is 87.96%, indicating that nearly 88 out of every 100 actual UHI locations are correctly identified, with approximately 12% of true hotspots missed. The F1-score is 0.8946, representing the harmonic mean of precision and recall. The ROC-AUC is 0.9850, demonstrating excellent discriminative ability across all probability thresholds. Cross-validated F1 across all five folds is 0.9435 ± 0.0153, indicating stable performance with low variance across different city partitions.


From the estimated confusion matrix (approximately 977 test samples with 44.94% positive rate, yielding approximately 439 positive and 538 negative samples): approximately 487 true negatives and 51 false positives in the negative class, and approximately 53 false negatives and 386 true positives in the positive class. In the context of urban planning applications, false negatives (failing to detect a genuine UHI hotspot) are arguably more costly than false positives, as undetected hotspots represent missed opportunities for potentially life-saving interventions. The system's recall of 87.96% under strict spatial cross-validation provides a strong safety margin, though future iterations may benefit from threshold calibration to further minimise false negatives at a modest precision cost.

### B. Regressor Performance

The Random Forest regressor (300 trees, no depth limit) achieves R² = 0.9416 on the 20% held-out test set, indicating that the seven-feature model explains 94.16% of the variance in absolute LST across the global dataset. The root mean squared error (RMSE) is ±2.0828°C, and the mean absolute error (MAE) is ±1.4117°C. Cross-validated RMSE across five folds is 4.6622°C, reflecting the increased difficulty of generalising temperature predictions across diverse climatic zones when entire city groups are held out. In the context of urban planning, an RMSE of ±2.08°C is within the range of operational utility: it is sufficiently precise to distinguish between UHI severity levels (mild: 1–3°C, moderate: 3–5°C, severe: >5°C) and to provide meaningful temperature projections for mitigation simulation, while acknowledging that satellite-derived LST inherently differs from air temperature measurements by 2–5°C depending on surface emissivity and atmospheric conditions.

### C. Feature Importance Analysis

The classifier's Gini importance ranking reveals that lst_delta dominates at 81.93%, confirming that the urban-rural thermal differential is the single most informative signal for UHI detection. This dominance is physically meaningful: UHI is, by definition, a relative thermal phenomenon, and lst_delta directly quantifies this relativity. The remaining five features contribute secondary information that collectively improves classification accuracy by approximately 12 percentage points over a univariate lst_delta model: elevation (4.80%) captures topographic cooling gradients where higher-altitude urban areas experience reduced UHI intensity, evi (3.76%) detects dense canopy cooling, ndbi (3.38%) quantifies built-up thermal mass, ndvi (3.08%) provides a general vegetation density signal, and ntl (3.06%) captures anthropogenic waste heat. The regressor's importance landscape is qualitatively different: rural_lst_mean dominates at 77.92% because absolute temperature prediction is fundamentally anchored by the regional climate baseline, with spectral indices (ndvi at 4.32%, ndbi at 4.28%), geographic factors (abs_lat at 4.48%), anthropogenic heat (ntl at 4.00%), topography (elevation at 3.03%), and dense vegetation (evi at 1.97%) modulating the urban thermal increment.

### D. Comparative Analysis: Version 1 versus Version 2

A systematic comparison between UHIS v1 and v2 demonstrates substantial improvements across all metrics and architectural dimensions. The classifier accuracy improves from 73.11% to 93.49% (+20.38 percentage points), precision from 70.52% to 91.00% (+20.48 percentage points), recall from 75.28% to 87.96% (+12.68 percentage points), and F1-score from 0.7282 to 0.8946 (+16.64 percentage points). ROC-AUC is newly measured at 0.9850 (not computed in v1). The regressor improves R² from 0.9072 to 0.9416 (+3.44 percentage points), RMSE from ±2.63°C to ±2.08°C (−20.9% error reduction), and MAE from ±1.79°C to ±1.41°C (−21.2% error reduction). The dataset expands from approximately 927 samples (15 per city) to 4,882 samples (80 per city), providing a five-fold increase in training data density.

The architectural improvements driving these gains span six dimensions. The classifier algorithm transitions from RandomForest to XGBoost, which provides superior gradient-boosted sequential learning with regularisation. The classifier feature space expands from three features (lst, ndvi, ndbi) to six features (lst_delta, ndvi, ndbi, evi, elevation, ntl), capturing anthropogenic and topographic drivers previously omitted. The regressor feature space expands from three features (ndvi, ndbi, abs_lat) to seven features (ndvi, ndbi, evi, elevation, ntl, abs_lat, rural_lst_mean). UHI labelling transitions from a fixed 2.0°C threshold to climate-zone-adaptive thresholds spanning 1.5–3.5°C. Cross-validation transitions from random splitting to GroupKFold by city, eliminating spatial leakage. Explainability advances from global Gini importance to real-time per-prediction SHAP values. The replacement of raw LST with lst_delta in the classifier feature vector eliminates the label leakage that artificially inflated v1 accuracy estimates.

## IX. Conclusions and Future Work

This paper has presented the Urban Heat Intelligence System (UHIS), a production-deployed geospatial intelligence platform that addresses five critical deficiencies in existing UHI detection and mitigation approaches. The five key contributions are as follows. First, the lst_delta innovation — replacing raw LST with a climate-zone-adaptive urban-rural thermal differential — eliminates label leakage and provides a physics-grounded UHI signal that is comparable across diverse climatic regimes. Second, GroupKFold spatial cross-validation by city prevents the spatial autocorrelation leakage that inflates accuracy estimates in conventional geospatial ML evaluations, yielding honest and reproducible performance metrics (93.49% accuracy, 0.9850 ROC-AUC). Third, real-time SHAP TreeExplainer integration delivers exact per-prediction feature attributions during inference, transforming the classifier from a black-box binary label into a transparent causal decomposition that urban planners can directly interpret and act upon. Fourth, the dual-mode counterfactual simulation engine — combining deterministic literature-grounded cooling bounds with non-linear ML recalculation — enables interactive what-if analysis of urban planning interventions, bridging the gap between UHI detection and actionable mitigation. Fifth, the complete system is deployed as a production-grade web application with concurrent satellite data extraction, sub-second ML inference, and a mission-control visual interface, demonstrating that research-grade geospatial ML can be operationalised for real-world use by domain experts.

Future work will pursue several directions to extend the capabilities and accuracy of the UHIS platform. Temporal dynamics integration will extend the system from single-snapshot analysis to seasonal and diurnal UHI monitoring via time-series Landsat composites, enabling trend detection and early warning systems. Deep learning classifiers, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and Vision Transformers (ViTs), will be evaluated on raw 10 m Sentinel-2 image patches to determine whether spatial context within satellite imagery can surpass hand-crafted spectral index features. Building-level thermal profiling through integration of high-resolution LiDAR data will enable individual structure–level UHI assessment, supporting targeted retrofitting recommendations. IoT-based model calibration will connect ground-truth weather station measurements to the satellite-derived pipeline, enabling continuous model recalibration and quantification of the satellite-to-air temperature gap. Finally, economic cost-benefit modelling will augment mitigation recommendations with estimated implementation costs, energy savings, and health impact valuations, enabling planners to optimise intervention portfolios under budget constraints.
